_The Iliad_ by Homer is an ancient Greek epic poem which details part of the final year of the Trojan War, a decades long conflict between Greeks and Trojans. The poem focuses on the rage of the greatest Greek warrior, Achilles, and its effects. While the epic mainly focuses on male characters, it provides insight into the roles of mortal women and goddesses in ancient Greek society. In the _Iliad_, mortal women are treated as objects to be fought over, rarely having their voices be heard. However, the goddesses have great influence on plot, with dialogue and important roles. This distinction between their roles indicates that mortal women and goddesses are not alike. Goddesses, due to their divinity, possess independence, agency, and power, allowing them to transcend mortal gender boundaries. This distinction reveals the constraints forced upon mortal women, while simultaneously revealing the roles of the divine.  
&nbsp;

## Independence

In the _Iliad_, mortal women are defined by their relationships to men, while goddesses are independent. Both the entire Trojan War and the central conflict of the _Iliad_ is characterized by who possesses women. Felson and Slatkin describe this phenomenon as, "the struggle over Chryseis [entailing] the struggle over Briseis, both of which follow upon the struggle over Helen" (95). Mortal women drive the story, not because they are individuals, but because of their relationship to men. Mortal women's complete dependence on men is seen when Andromache, defined by her marriage to Hector, begs her husband to, "not make your son an orphan and your wife a widow" (_Il._ VI. 430-440). Andromache is completely reliant on Hector as she cannot flee with her own son, even if it means saving both of their lives. Her existence cannot exist independent of her marriage, even after her husband's death, as Hector predicts, "And sometimes people. . . will say, 'That woman used to be the wife of Hector. . .'" (_Il._ VI. 460-470). By defining mortal women to their relation to men, and not vice versa, mortal women are constrained to complete dependence on men.  
&nbsp;  
Goddesses are completely opposites in this aspect. Their existence is dictated by their relation to a god, Zeus, but so are the gods. The gods and goddesses are equal in independence, regardless of their gender, as goddesses are not defined differently from gods. Mortal women are physically constrained to their respective camps, while mortal men move from their camps to the battlefield regularly. In contrast, goddesses repeatedly move freely and in the same manner as gods do. This is easily seen in book five, when the goddesses Aphrodite, Athena, and Hera all simultaneously participate on the battlefield, along with the gods Apollo and Ares do. Goddesses' movement on the battlefield is the norm, as even Zeus tells Athena, a goddess, "Hurry to the armies and join the Greeks and Trojans" (_Il._ V. 60-70). Goddesses' independent movement starkly contrasts mortal women, whose movement is bound, as they can only move through men (whether through capture, instruction, or force).  
&nbsp;

## Agency

The independence goddesses possess grants them far more agency than any mortal woman. With mortal women being possessed as if they were objects, what little agency they possess is used discreetly. In comparison, goddesses move and act freely, with little constraining them. This distinction is seen in the interaction between Helen, a mortal woman, and Aphrodite, a goddess. In book three, Aphrodite summons Helen to Paris' bedchamber, to which Helen has no choice but to follow. Aphrodite is in control of Helen, while Helen has no control over her own situation. Helen cannot decide her fate, but Aphrodite and the other goddesses can, by influencing the war or by directly ordering her. Helen expresses to Aphrodite very clearly she does not want to attend to Paris, but Aphrodite threatens, "I shall devise a strategy to make you loathed and abhorred by both the Greeks and Trojans, and you shall die a dreadful death" (_Il._ III. 410-420). Aphrodite's threat makes light of her agency and Helen's lack of agency, as Felson and Slatkin explain, "[Mortal] women's anger is so foreign to Iliadic representation that it almost never appears, as if it were, or should be unimaginable."  
&nbsp;  
Felson and Slatkin note that mortal women only truly express their feelings and wants while mourning, even then, "these hopes are defined by husband and children." Uniquely, Helen is the only mortal woman who showcases, "autonomous agency," but even then it is, "in relation to her expectations" (97). This phenomenon reveals mortal women's bondage to men, even if it shows Helen is acutely aware of the boundaries forced upon her by her gender. These boundaries are seen when Briseis, Achilles' war prize returned by Agamemnon, mourns Patroclus, in her first and last lines. Her voice, only permitted when mourning a man, is reflective of her lack of agency. Briseis, and other mortal women, are only allowed to speak when mourning because it has no impact. A dead man will not come back to life, therefore women's expressions of grief are permitted because it has no use. In comparison, whether it be Hera tricking Zeus in book fourteen, or Athena accompanying Diomedes in book five, goddesses regularly act and speak freely, with their words and actions being of great consequence. Goddesses freely influence the war and use their voices, while mortal women are constrained from self expression and consequential decisions.  
&nbsp;

## Power

Finally, mortal women are barred from power, while goddesses use power freely. Mortal women are completely powerless, with no way to escape their situation, no matter how much they despise it. The slave woman Briseis has no freedom, nor does she have any say in who possesses her; same with the other countless unnamed slave women. Their only hope is to wait for a man, e.g. their father or relative, to save them, or to be married to a man who treats them well. The only modicum of 'power' mortal women possess is through convincing mortal men to help them in their endeavors or by praying to the divine. However, these forms of 'power' are not true power; they are merely a reflection of how mortal women are coerced into relying further upon men or the divine, in light of their oppression. This is not real power as there is no guarantee it has any effect, especially not the individual action guarantees effect, no matter how small. This is clearly demonstrated in book six, when Hecuba prays to Athena, yet Athena, "refused to grant her prayer" (_Il._ VI. 410-420). This interaction plainly reveals the power dynamic between mortal women, and goddesses. Mortal women have no power, and can only beg, in comparison, goddesses are the deciding factor in whether or not their pleas are answered. Through power, goddesses surpass the constraints forced upon mortal women.  
&nbsp;  
Although the power gap between mortal men and women is large, there is virtually no power gap between goddesses and gods based on gender. The similarity between gods, and goddesses' power is seen in book 5, when comparing Aphrodite to Apollo. During battle, both Aphrodite and Apollo have the power to save their preferred soldiers. Unlike with mortals, where only men are on the battlefield, both gods and goddesses participate. When Aphrodite is injured in battle, she is told, "My child, the work of war is not your area," by Zeus. Principally, it is not because Aphrodite is a goddess, and not a god, that Zeus tells her this; it is because he believes Aphrodite specifically is not suited to war, as he also states, "fierce Ares and Athena will attend to all of that," making it clear he believes both gods and goddesses can fight in battle, and possess equal power (_Il._ V. 425-431). Unlike mortal women, goddesses possess and utilize equal power to their male counterparts with no qualms.  
&nbsp;

## Conclusion

In the _Iliad_, goddesses, due to their divinity, possess independence, agency, and power, allowing them to transcend mortal gender boundaries. This transcendence reveals gender roles within the _Iliad_ and Homer's understandings of divinity. What goddesses have that mortal women lack highlights the roles and positions expected of mortal women, while demonstrating their constraints, even when resisted.
